Part 1 · With an experiment

What did the ads cause, and for whom?

Four studies read the same randomized experiment on 14.0 million users: the effect, the effect by segment, the definitions and checks behind the numbers, and segment effects when the counts carry privacy noise.

01a

How many site visits did the ads cause, as opposed to visits that would have happened anyway?

Assignment to the ad group raised the visit rate from 3.82% to 4.85% and the conversion rate from 0.19% to 0.31%. The pre-specified uplift model did not beat a plain response model at ranking users for targeting.

01b

Can segment-level ad effects be read from a 2% sample?

Across ten 2% samples, a Bayesian multilevel model cut the error of 100 segment estimates by 27% against separate per-segment estimates, in 10 of 10 samples. Its 90% intervals are conservative (96% coverage).

01c

Is every number of the readout defined, checked and reproducible from the raw file?

Every number in the readout comes from one short SQL query with a stated numerator and denominator, and agrees with the Python pipeline of studies 01a and 02a in 29 of 29 comparisons. The file has no user identifier, so a user is a row, and 16% of rows are identical to another row.

01d

If segment counts are released with differential-privacy noise, how much accuracy is left?

With Laplace noise at epsilon = 1, a multilevel model that knows the noise scale is as accurate (1.06 points) as per-segment estimates on the unprotected counts (1.08). A second test in the same plan did not meet its rule: ranking segments by estimated lift beat ranking by response rate at 10% and 20% budgets, but not at 30%.
