Part 1 · With an experiment

What did the ads cause, and for whom?

Criteo ran the experiments. I used its public release of 13.98 million user records to estimate lift, test whom to target and audit the readout.

01a

How many site visits did the ads cause, as opposed to visits that would have happened anyway?

Assignment to the ad-eligible group raised the visit rate from 3.82% to 4.85% (+1.03 pp; 0.77 pp adjusted) and the conversion rate from 0.194% to 0.309%. Only 3.6% of the eligible group recorded an exposure. The pre-specified uplift model did not beat a plain response model at ranking users for targeting.

01b

Can segment-level ad effects be read from a 2% sample?

Across ten 2% samples, a Bayesian multilevel model reduced the mean error of 100 segment estimates from 1.08 to 0.79 pp against separate estimates; error fell in every sample. Its 90% intervals contained the held-out estimate in 96% of segment–sample comparisons.

01c

Is every number of the readout defined, checked and reproducible from the raw file?

Every number in the readout comes from one short SQL query with a stated numerator and denominator, and agrees with the Python pipeline of studies 01a and 02a in 29 of 29 comparisons. The file has no user identifier, so a user is a row, and 16% of rows are identical to another row.

01d

If segment counts are released with differential-privacy noise, how much accuracy is left?

With Laplace noise at ε = 1, mean error was 1.06 pp for the noise-aware multilevel model, 1.08 for separate estimates without noise, and 0.95 for the same multilevel model without noise. A second test did not meet its rule: ranking segments by estimated lift beat ranking by response rate at 10% and 20% reach, but only in 4 of 10 samples at 30%.
