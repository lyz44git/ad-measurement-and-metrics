Part 3 · Planning a test

How large must the test be, and what is the baseline?

Studies 03a and 03b size the next experiment, by user and by market; in both, adjusting for what is known before the test is the main way to make it smaller. Study 03c forecasts the baseline of a metric so that national, state and metro levels agree. Study 03d asks whether a test can be made smaller by reading the effect on conversions from visits.

03a

How many users per arm does a lift study need, and how much does covariate adjustment save?

For a one-point lift in site visits on a 3.8% base rate, an unadjusted readout needs about 6,500 users per arm for 80% power. Adjusting for the 12 user features recorded before the ad cuts this to about 4,700, and adjusting for a model prediction built from them to about 4,400, with false-positive rates still near 5%.

03b

If a change can only be randomized by market, how large a test is needed, and does the usual t-test keep its 5% false-positive rate?

In placebo tests on historical metro listing counts, regression adjustment on pre-period volume lowered the smallest detectable effect of a two-month, 100-metro test from 9.5% (year-over-year growth) to 6.7%; matched pairs added nothing once the estimate was adjusted. For the volume-weighted effect on log listings, randomization inference kept the false-positive rate between 4.2% and 5.8%, where the t-test reached 9.1%.

03c

Can the baseline of a metric be forecast at national, state and metro levels so that the levels add up, without losing accuracy?

Forecasts made separately for the US, divisions, states and metros disagree: each of the three parent levels (US, divisions, states) differs from the sum of the forecasts one level below it by 3.5% to 5.2%. Bottom-up reconciliation removes the gap at no cost in accuracy. MinT-shrink lowered state and metro error with this base model, but the gain did not hold with a second model or target, so bottom-up is the safe default here.

03d

Can the effect on visits stand in for the effect on conversions?

Not for the size of the effect, but for where it is. An estimate that converts the extra visits at the rate predicted for visitors of their kind is 42% of the directly measured effect on conversions, and a third once both are adjusted for the features; the pre-specified ±20% margin was not met. Across 100 user segments, though, the two effects move together, and in a test of 2% of the users choosing segments by their visit effect picks a 15% larger conversion effect than choosing by conversions, in 20 of 20 samples.
