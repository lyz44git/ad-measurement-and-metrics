Part 3 · Planning a test

What would it take to detect a change worth acting on?

Study 03a resamples the Criteo visit data to size a user-level test. Study 03b injects hypothetical changes into Zillow's historical counts of newly pending listings to size a test where whole markets are assigned; no ads were served. In both, adjusting for pre-test data is the main way to make a test smaller. Study 03c switches to for-sale inventory and tests whether local forecasts can add up to the national total.

03a

How many users per arm does a lift study need, and how much does covariate adjustment save?

To detect a rise in visit rate from about 3.8% to 4.8% with 80% power (two-sided 5% test), the estimated requirement is 6,458 users per arm unadjusted, 4,682 adjusting for the 12 pre-test user features, and 4,430 adjusting for a model prediction built from them, with A/A false-positive rates near 5%.

03b

If a change can only be randomized by market, how large a test is needed, and does the usual t-test keep its 5% false-positive rate?

In placebo tests on 151 eligible metros' newly pending listings, regression adjustment on pre-period volume lowered the smallest detectable change of a two-month, 100-metro test (50 per group) from 9.5% (year-over-year growth) to 6.7%; pairing then changed it to 6.8%. For total volume in two-month tests, the volume-weighted t-test flagged up to 9.1% of placebo tests and randomization inference 4.2% to 5.8%.

03c

Can local inventory forecasts add up to the national total without losing accuracy?

Separate forecasts for the US, divisions, states and metros fail to add up by 3.5% to 5.2% on average. Adding up local forecasts (bottom-up) closes every gap by construction. Forecast error matched or improved on separate forecasts in 11 of 12 comparisons; the exception was national new listings, where error rose 0.7%. MinT-shrink lowered state and metro error with this base model, but the gain did not hold with a second model or target, so bottom-up is the safe default here.
