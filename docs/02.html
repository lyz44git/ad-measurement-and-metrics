Part 2 · Without an experiment

When can the usual metrics be trusted?

Studies 02a and 02c check a metric used in place of an experiment against a benchmark: the experiment of study 01a, or a simulation with known returns. Study 02b shows how much the credit given to each touch depends on the rule chosen, and study 02d whether that changes what a bidder buys. Study 02e checks estimates of what a new targeting rule would bring against a benchmark from the experiment, and study 02f checks conversion forecasts made before the labels arrive against the labels that arrive later.

02a

Can an exposure-based attribution metric be trusted as a measure of ad impact?

Exposure-based attribution credited 23,031 conversions in the ad group; from the random assignment, the experiment puts the conversions caused by the ads at an estimated 13,687 on the released sample (about 11,902 after regression adjustment), so the count is 68% higher than the unadjusted estimate. The release pools several tests with non-uniform subsampling. Without an experiment, covariate adjustment moved the per-exposed-user visit effect from a naive 38.0 points to 24.6–25.5, between the two experimental estimates; these are associations within the ad arm, while the experimental estimates are complier effects, so the band is descriptive, not a bound.

02b

When a user sees several ads before converting, how much does the credit rule change which touch gets credit?

65% of converting paths have several touches, and last touch gives no credit to 82% of the touches in them. A time-decay rule fitted to observed touch-to-conversion delays leaves 39% of the credit on the last touch. These rules split credit; they do not show which touches caused the conversion.

02c

Can a marketing mix model recover each channel's return from a brand's own spend and revenue, and can one tell in advance when it will?

A marketing mix model recovers a channel's return only where that channel's spend moves enough on its own, and this can be checked before fitting: the median error was 19% for brand-channels with a signal-to-noise index of 2 or more and 50% for the rest. Only 19% of brand-channels reach that level on weekly data, against 55% on daily data.

02d

If a bidder values impressions by expected credit, does the attribution rule change what it buys?

Hardly, in this log. At half the logged budget the last-touch and time-decay bidders share 90% of their spend, and a bidder trained on one rule gives up at most 2.2% of the other rule's credit, below the pre-specified 5% threshold. What moves is the marginal spend: the time-decay bidder buys fewer impressions shortly after a click.

02e

What is a targeting policy worth before it has run?

It can be read from a biased log if the log is large enough. On logs of about 1.6 million users that favor likely visitors, where an unweighted comparison gives 3 to 4 times the lift, the doubly robust estimator, which has to estimate the probability of assignment, is off by 2.5% to 2.7% of a feature-adjusted benchmark from the experiment in a single log, and inverse weighting with the recorded probability by 3.0% to 3.9%. Both pre-specified rules were met. Under strong bias the doubly robust estimate stays within 6% while inverse weighting with an estimated probability falls about a third short.

The experiment's value of each policy, adjusted and unadjusted

In this pooled release the chance of being assigned to ads varies slightly with the features. The value that treats the assignment share as the same for every user is 34% to 42% higher than the value adjusted for the features, which this study uses as its benchmark.

The same gap explains why study 01a's adjusted effect on visits (0.77 points) is below its unadjusted one (1.03). A first design that took the unadjusted value as its benchmark did not meet its rule.

02f

Can a conversion model trained before its labels arrive be corrected for the delay?

The level, yes. Retrained each day on the conversions seen so far, a network predicts 11.5% too few conversions within 14 days. A delayed-feedback network in PyTorch, which also learns how conversions are spread over the 14 days, predicts 1.8% too many, against 0.0% for a network trained on complete labels, and campaign by campaign it lowers the forecast error from 16.0% to 13.3% (11.7% with complete labels); both pre-specified rules were met. Its log loss did not improve: it fixes the level and ranks slightly less well. Leaving out the last two weeks to wait for complete labels ranks much worse.
